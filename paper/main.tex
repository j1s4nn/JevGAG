\title[mode=title]{Regime-Aware Temporal Granularity for Causal Video World Models:\\
When Does Adaptivity Pay?}

\author[1]{Hossen Md Jisan}[orcid=0009-0004-9571-1450]
\affiliation[1]{organization={Department of Artificial Intelligence, Nanjing University of
Information Science and Technology (NUIST)}, city={Nanjing}, country={China}}
\cortext[1]{Corresponding author.}
\ead{jisanahammed1952@gmail.com}

\begin{abstract}
Causal video generators produce a rollout one temporal chunk at a time, so spending expensive per-frame
compute only on hard segments looks attractive. We show that this idea rests on three
\emph{preconditions} --- cost, signal and endpoint --- and that all three are \emph{regime properties}
rather than universal facts. On a matched two-GPU study (Tesla T4 and A100-80GB) of a causal diffusion
backbone we (i) measure the per-forward cost against the schedule break-even and find the same mode pair
on opposite sides of the inequality; (ii) show that hand-crafted context statistics cannot predict coarse-unsafe
segments (AUROC 0.491), that an \emph{intrinsic} probe on the generator's own prefill activations reaches
0.798 on one GPU and \emph{inverts} to 0.143 on the other, and that only a \emph{multi-feature} model over
the probe and a sampler convergence residual transfers across both (0.842 and 0.833); and (iii) show that
an endpoint independent of the compared modes is mandatory, because the internal reference reverses
between GPUs. We then derive a \emph{regime map} that states exactly when adaptivity pays --- only when
the cheap mode is cheaper \emph{and} worse --- for a stated objective. Across two GPUs (the latent
endpoint) and on T4 for two further endpoints (decoded-pixel and temporal flicker), our operating points
fall outside that region: the coarse mode was never worse in quality, so under the stated objective the
optimal policy is a \emph{fixed} mode and a router is unnecessary. We also report a training-free seam repair that reduces the switch-boundary discontinuity by
25\% while improving fidelity, quantify drift propagation, and record three boundaries: no single signal
transfers across GPUs, drift is linear and policy-insensitive, and a pixel-only decision-model critic
is at chance. All results, figures and audited result files are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Interactive video world models --- game engines, robot simulators, embodied agents and augmented
reality --- must synthesise frames under a latency budget on whatever accelerator is available. A causal
(autoregressive) video generator emits the rollout one temporal chunk at a time, and chunks are not
equally hard. It is therefore natural to spend \emph{fine} per-frame computation on difficult segments
and a cheaper \emph{coarse} multi-frame mode elsewhere, deciding before each segment is generated.

This idea is appealing but conditional. Our earlier study~\citep{hossen2026jevRouted} framed it as
resting on three \emph{preconditions}: (P1) a cheap mode must be genuinely cheaper on the deployment
hardware at the chosen schedule; (P2) a signal available \emph{before} generation must predict which
segments are unsafe for the cheap mode; and (P3) the quality claim must be certified by an endpoint that
is independent of the modes being compared. That study measured the preconditions on a single GPU and
reported a conditional trade-off.

This paper asks the question that follows: \emph{when does adaptivity actually pay?} We answer with a
matched two-GPU study (Tesla T4 and A100-80GB) of a causal diffusion backbone, and we make four
contributions.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A two-GPU regime map} (\S\ref{sec:method}) that partitions the
  (cost-ratio, quality-gap) plane into three policies --- fixed fine, fixed coarse and adaptive --- and
  states precisely when a router is worth deploying. The adaptive region is defined by a conjunction:
  the cheap mode must be \emph{cheaper} \emph{and} \emph{worse}.
  \item \textbf{An intrinsic signal --- and the limit of single signals.} A probe on the generator's own
  prefill activations predicts coarse-unsafe segments at AUROC 0.798 with no external oracle, against
  0.491 for hand-crafted context statistics. The same probe \emph{inverts} on the second GPU (0.143,
  CI $[0.018, 0.368]$), while a free sampler \emph{convergence residual} (the ratio of the first to the
  last denoising-step update magnitude) merely \emph{falls to chance} there (0.760 on T4, 0.404 on A100,
  whose interval $[0.223, 0.603]$ contains $0.5$). Only a \emph{multi-feature} model over the two
  transfers across both GPUs (0.842 and 0.833).
  \item \textbf{A training-free seam repair.} Switching modes creates a measurable discontinuity at the
  boundary. Blending the first latent frame of the new segment toward the boundary by 25\% reduces the
  seam by 25\% \emph{and} improves fidelity, with no training.
  \item \textbf{A boundary analysis.} We show, and record, that signals are regime properties: the
  intrinsic probe \emph{inverts} on the second GPU (0.798~$\rightarrow$~0.143). Drift is linear and not
  predictable from the policy, and a decision-model critic shown only pixels is at chance. Each boundary
  is stated with the experiment that produced it.
\end{itemize}

A summary of the programme is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}. Throughout, we report the \emph{measured}
limit of each naive formulation rather than treating it as a failure; the boundaries are the paper's
design output.

\paragraph{Relationship to the prior study.}
The prior preprint~\citep{hossen2026jevRouted} measured the three preconditions on a \emph{single} GPU
with an \emph{external} decision-model oracle. This paper is a distinct study: it extends the measurement
to two GPUs, replaces the external oracle with an intrinsic signal, adds the convergence residual and the
seam repair, and derives the regime map. The audit summarised in \S\ref{sec:background} (the
\emph{report}) is an unpublished artifact of the accompanying repository; no text or figure of the prior
preprint is reused here.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Causal and autoregressive video generation.}
Streaming video generators produce a rollout chunk by chunk, and the train--test gap between teacher
forcing and self-rolling is a central difficulty~\citep{huang2025self,zhu2026causal,zhao2026causal}.
Recent recipes distil few-step causal diffusion models for interactive
use~\citep{zheng2026causalrcm,zhao2026minwm,ai2025magi1}. Our backbone is one of this family; we treat
it as a black box and study its \emph{granularity}, not its training.

\paragraph{Adaptive compute and routing.}
Adaptive computation was introduced for recurrent networks~\citep{graves2016adaptive}, early exiting for
classification~\citep{teerapittayanon2017branchynet}, and dynamic routing~\citep{wang2017skipnet}. In generative video, recent work
allocates computation by step or by token: dynamic step allocation~\citep{le2026dsa}, learned flexible
chunking~\citep{ma2026flexforcing}, pruning and routing~\citep{wang2026pare}, layer reuse~\citep{adnan2025foresight},
and caching of attention or flow state~\citep{ma2026flow,xu2026motionaware,wang2026inflight,song2026trac}.
Adaptive tokenisation changes the \emph{representation}~\citep{li2025learning,xiong2026evatok,yuan2025dlfrvae,zhong2025vfrtok}.
Our work differs in object and in method: we route the \emph{temporal granularity} of a fixed backbone,
and --- crucially --- we do not assume that routing pays; we characterise the regime in which it does.

\paragraph{Internal probes and verification.}
Ensembles of internal classifiers and early-exit policies read a network's own intermediate
representations~\citep{sun2021early,xu2023lgvit,yoo2026adept,zeng2023consistentee,zhou2020bert}. We adapt this
idea to \emph{generation-mode safety}: a probe on a diffusion transformer's prefill activations predicts
whether a cheap mode is unsafe for the upcoming segment. Speculative decoding is
related~\citep{chen2026dflash,liu2024pearl,li2026is}, but speculates over tokens; here the axis is
temporal chunking.

\paragraph{Evaluation of generative video.}
Video generative models are evaluated along many axes, including temporal
flickering~\citep{huang2024vbench,zheng2025vbench20}, artefact taxonomies~\citep{zhu2026artifactaware}, and
compression-aware consistency~\citep{zsoldos2026evaluating}. We adopt a multi-endpoint protocol: a latent
reference, a decoded-pixel reference and a temporal-flicker statistic, so that a claim cannot be an
artefact of one metric.

\paragraph{Decision models.}
System One decision models return typed, calibrated answers rather than generated
text~\citep{ling2026jev,geuter2026releasing,li2026llmasjev}. We refer to the decision model used here and
in the earlier study as \emph{Jev} (a System One model, \texttt{jev-1.14.0}). The earlier study used Jev
as an off-device routing oracle; here we test it as a \emph{critic} and report the boundary we find,
consistent with independent evidence that pixel-only quality prediction with such models is
unreliable~\citep{robitza2026jevqa}.

\section{Background: the three preconditions}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Setting.}
A causal generator with two granularity modes is rolled out over $T$ segments. A \emph{fine} mode emits
one latent frame per chunk; a \emph{coarse} mode emits $K$ frames jointly. A policy chooses a mode per
segment. We write $c_f, c_c$ for the per-segment cost of the two modes and $q_f, q_c$ for a quality
functional (lower is better). We write $s_a/s_b$ for a \emph{schedule pair}: the fine generator runs $a$
denoising steps per latent frame and the coarse generator $b$ steps per chunk. The one-frame-per-chunk
fine mode is written $c_{1\text{-}1}$ and the three-frame-per-chunk coarse mode $c_{3\text{-}3}$; both run
two steps by default, so the matched two-step pair is $s_2/s_2$, and a two-step-fine / one-step-coarse
pair is $s_2/s_1$.

\paragraph{The three preconditions.}
\begin{description}
  \item[P1 (cost).] The cheap mode must satisfy $c_c < c_f$ on the deployment hardware at the chosen
  schedule. Because a coarse forward is heavier than a fine forward, the condition is a comparison of the
  measured per-forward ratio against the \emph{break-even} set by the number of function evaluations of
  each schedule, $\rho_\text{be} = \mathrm{NFE}_f / \mathrm{NFE}_c$. Coarse is cheaper iff the measured
  per-forward ratio $\pi$ is \emph{below} $\rho_\text{be}$.\footnote{For the matched two-step pair
  ($\mathrm{NFE}_f = 34$, $\mathrm{NFE}_c = 12.67$) $\rho_\text{be} = 2.68$; for the two-step-fine /
  one-step-coarse pair ($\mathrm{NFE}_c = 8.33$) $\rho_\text{be} = 4.08$. The prior study's single-step
  break-even ($2.33$) is a different schedule and is not used for any claim in this paper.}
  \item[P2 (signal).] A quantity computable \emph{before} a segment is generated must separate segments
  for which the cheap mode is unsafe. The earlier study found that hand-crafted context statistics fail
  this test and that an off-device decision model passes it.
  \item[P3 (endpoint).] The quality claim must be certified by a measure that is independent of the two
  modes. The earlier study found that an internal reference reverses direction between GPUs.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Matched design and label.}
For every prompt and segment we generate \emph{both} modes from the same boundary and the same noise, so
that the only difference is the generator. A segment is \emph{coarse-unsafe} when the coarse segment is
farther from a high-quality reference (a four-step fine rollout) than the fine segment, and the label is
$y = \mathbb{1}[\,d(m_c) > d(m_f)\,]$, with $d(\cdot)$ the endpoint distance of a generated segment
$m \in \{\text{fine},\text{coarse}\}$. The symbol $q$ denotes the \emph{expectation} of $d$, so
$q_f = \mathbb{E}[d(m_f)]$.

\paragraph{The report.}
We call the audit of the preconditions the \emph{JevGAG report}, and the regime-aware method of
\S\ref{sec:method} \emph{DDAG} (Deadline- and Drift-Aware Adaptive Temporal Granularity); the title
frames the same method as \emph{regime-aware}. On T4 the report finds that coarse is \emph{not} cheaper
for a two-step coarse schedule, that hand-crafted context statistics reach only 0.491 AUROC while the Jev
text oracle reaches 0.788, and that the internal reference flips between GPUs. On A100 the cost ordering
reverses. The report also shows that a two-threshold controller with a hysteresis band and a
forced-recovery window stabilises switching, and that switching leaves a measurable \emph{seam}. Full
details, code and audited result files are in the repository accompanying this paper.

\section{Method: regime-aware granularity}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The regime map}
\label{sec:regimemap}

Let $r = c_c / c_f$ be the cost ratio and $\Delta = q_c - q_f$ the quality gap, with $\Delta > 0$ meaning
the cheap mode is worse. \textbf{Objective.} A policy is evaluated under the constrained objective
\begin{equation}
\text{minimise } \mathbb{E}[\text{DiT seconds}] \quad \text{subject to} \quad
\mathbb{E}[d] \le \mathbb{E}[d \mid \text{fixed fine}],
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ spend as little as possible without degrading the endpoint relative to always generating fine.
Under Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective} the plane is partitioned into three policies:
\begin{equation}
\text{policy} =
\begin{cases}
\text{fixed fine}, & r > 1 \quad \text{(the cheap mode is dearer)},\\
\text{adaptive},   & r < 1 \ \wedge\ \Delta > 0 \quad \text{(cheaper and worse)},\\
\text{fixed coarse}, & r < 1 \ \wedge\ \Delta \le 0 \quad \text{(cheaper and no worse)}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:regime}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:regime} is the method's core: a router is worth deploying \emph{only} in the middle
region. Outside it, one mode dominates and the routing machinery is pure overhead. Figure~\ref{fig:regime}
plots our operating points in this plane. At two of our three operating points the cost-optimal and
quality-optimal modes coincide; at T4 $s_2/s_2$ they do not, because the fine mode is cheaper while the
coarse mode is closer to the reference. In every case the adaptive region is empty --- the coarse mode is
either dearer ($r>1$) or no worse ($\Delta\le0$) --- which is why the regime map is a statement about the
regime rather than about a particular objective.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{figures/fig_regime_map.pdf}
  \caption{The regime map. The adaptive region (upper left, shaded) is empty at every operating point we
  measured: across both GPUs the coarse mode was never worse in quality, so the optimal policy is a fixed
  mode. Markers are labelled with the schedule pair and the intrinsic-probe AUROC at that point.}
  \label{fig:regime}
\end{figure}

\subsection{An intrinsic pre-generation probe}
\label{sec:probe}

At the decision point for a segment, the generator has already encoded the boundary context. We capture
the pooled output of each transformer block of the single prefill forward on the boundary and fit a
logistic probe on a chosen block. The probe is a \emph{pre-generation} quantity: it uses only activations
the rollout has already computed, requires no external service, and adds no measurable latency. The probe
is evaluated with leave-one-prompt-out cross-validation, so no segment is scored by a model that has seen
its own prompt.

\subsection{A convergence residual}
\label{sec:crv}

A distilled few-step sampler has no explicit error estimate, but its update magnitudes are observable.
Let $v_1, \dots, v_S$ be the norm of the predicted velocity at each denoising step of a segment. We define
the \emph{convergence residual}
\begin{equation}
\kappa = v_1 / v_S ,
\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
the ratio of the \emph{first} to the \emph{last} step magnitude: a segment whose initial update is large
relative to its final one is a segment the coarse mode has not yet resolved. Because $\kappa$ is computed
from quantities the sampler already produces, it is free. Two cautions apply, and both matter for
reproducibility. First, $\kappa$ must be scored by its \emph{direct} AUROC: passing a single scalar
through a logistic regression re-learns its sign and silently reports $\max(\text{AUROC}, 1-\text{AUROC})$,
which hides an inverted signal. Second, a scalar and its reciprocal carry exactly complementary
information: $\mathrm{AUROC}(v_S/v_1) = 1 - \mathrm{AUROC}(\kappa)$ holds identically, so the reciprocal
$v_S/v_1$ is \emph{inverted by construction} on any data (direct AUROC $0.240$ on T4). The direction of a
convergence statistic therefore cannot be read off the quantity itself; the sign must be fixed by
definition and checked by direct AUROC.

\subsection{Calibrated gating}
\label{sec:gate}

Given a score $u$ that increases with expected unsafety, a gate escalates a segment to the fine mode when
$u > \tau$. Two regimes are distinguished. In \emph{routing} the gate chooses the mode before generation;
in \emph{escalation} the cheap mode is always generated and the fine mode is recomputed when the gate
fires. Let $c_p$ be the \emph{incremental} cost of obtaining the score once per segment. For the intrinsic
probe $c_p \approx 0$: the probe reads the prefill forward the rollout performs anyway (\S\ref{sec:probe}),
so it adds no measurable latency, whereas a score that needs a separate forward would cost $c_p = c_f$.
Escalation then costs $c_c + c_p + \rho c_f$ against $c_f$ for fixed fine, so a saving requires
$\rho < 1 - r - c_p/c_f$; routing costs $c_p + (1-\rho)c_c + \rho c_f$ and saves whenever
$r < 1 - c_p/\bigl((1-\rho)c_f\bigr)$. This is why the cost precondition, not the signal, is the binding
constraint.

\subsection{Training-free seam repair}
\label{sec:seam}

When the mode changes at a boundary, the first frame of the new segment is generated by a different
generator than the frame before it, and the adjacent-frame distance at that boundary exceeds the
interior. We repair it without training: the first latent frame of the incoming segment is blended toward
the boundary frame (Eq.~\eqref{eq:blend}),
\begin{equation}
\tilde{x}_1 = (1-\alpha)\,x_1 + \alpha\,b ,
\label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}
with $b$ the boundary latent and $\alpha$ a scalar. Because the first frame is blended a fraction
$\alpha$ toward the boundary, the boundary seam is $(1-\alpha)$ of its unrepaired value \emph{by
construction}; the empirical questions are therefore fidelity and flicker, not the seam itself. We sweep
$\alpha$ and report the seam--fidelity frontier.

\section{Experimental protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Backbone and hardware.}
A causal distilled diffusion video model (1.3B) at $480\times832$; a four-step fine rollout is the
reference. Two accelerators: a \textbf{Tesla T4} (Turing, SDPA attention) and an
\textbf{A100-80GB} (Ampere, FlashAttention-2). The same prompts, seed, horizon and code are used on both.

\paragraph{Prompts and horizon.}
Sixteen prompts spanning object, scene, motion and human-action strata; a 57-frame horizon with a shared
three-frame coarse prefix and four segments of three latent frames each. Drift experiments use an
81-frame horizon with six segments.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Leave-one-prompt-out cross-validation grouped by prompt; prompt-grouped bootstrap confidence intervals
(2{,}000--4{,}000 resamples); permutation nulls for AUROC. Costs are wall-clock DiT seconds measured with
device synchronisation around the sampling call.

\paragraph{Endpoints.}
Three independent endpoints: (i) latent $L_1$ to the reference; (ii) \emph{decoded-pixel} $L_1$ to the
reference; (iii) a temporal-flicker statistic, the mean absolute consecutive-frame difference. A claim
is accepted only if it holds on the endpoints that are independent of the compared modes.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Backbone: Causal-rCM Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B, upstream commit \texttt{ed3cb14}. Seed $17$ throughout. The probe
is a logistic model on the pooled output of transformer block $11$ of the prefill forward; it is
evaluated leave-one-prompt-out. Costs are wall-clock DiT seconds around the sampling call, measured with
device synchronisation. Table~\ref{tab:protocol} records the sample size and endpoint coverage of every
experiment; all result files, kernels and Colab runners are public.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Cost is hardware-conditional}
Two ratios must be kept apart. The \emph{per-forward} ratio $\pi$ (seconds per single forward, coarse vs.\
fine) is a hardware property; the \emph{per-segment cost} ratio $r = c_c/c_f$ used by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:regime} depends on the hardware and the schedule. In the matched two-step matrix,
$\pi = 2.97$ on T4 and $1.74$ on A100 against a break-even of $2.68$, so the \emph{same} mode pair sits on
opposite sides of the inequality on the two GPUs (the prior study's single-step pair gave $3.01$ and
$1.60$ against $2.33$). Consistently, $r = 1.030$ for T4 $s_2/s_2$ (coarse $3\%$ dearer), $0.728$ for
T4 $s_2/s_1$ (coarse $27\%$ cheaper) and $0.536$ for A100 $s_2/s_2$ (coarse $46\%$ cheaper). The cost
precondition is thus a property of the (schedule, accelerator) pair, as Eq.~\eqref{eq:regime} requires.

\subsection{An intrinsic signal, and the limit of single signals}
Figure~\ref{fig:signal} summarises the signal study; every value is a \emph{direct} AUROC with a 95\,\%
prompt-grouped bootstrap interval ($n = 64$ segments per GPU). Hand-crafted context statistics reach
$0.491$; the intrinsic prefill probe reaches $\mathbf{0.798}$ $[0.639, 0.916]$ on T4, the convergence
residual of Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa} reaches $\mathbf{0.760}$ $[0.629, 0.870]$, and a multi-feature model over
the two reaches $\mathbf{0.842}$ $[0.680, 0.938]$. The transfer result is the informative part: on A100
the probe \emph{inverts} to $0.143$ $[0.018, 0.368]$ and the residual merely \emph{falls to chance}, at
$0.404$ $[0.223, 0.603]$ (interval contains $0.5$), yet the \emph{combined} model still reaches
$\mathbf{0.833}$ $[0.605, 0.971]$ --- no single signal transfers, the combination does. Two cautions: the
reciprocal $v_S/v_1$ is inverted \emph{by construction} ($\mathrm{AUROC}(v_S/v_1) =
1-\mathrm{AUROC}(\kappa)$ identically), giving $0.240$ $[0.130, 0.371]$ on T4; and a scalar signal must
never be scored through a single-feature logistic, which re-learns the sign and hides any inversion.

  \centering

  \caption{Signal study: direct AUROCs with 95\,\% prompt-grouped bootstrap intervals ($n = 64$ segments
  per GPU). On T4 the intrinsic probe (0.798) and the convergence residual (0.760) both beat hand-crafted
  context statistics (0.491), and their combination reaches 0.842. On A100 the probe \emph{inverts}
  (0.143) and the residual drops to 0.404, yet the combination still reaches 0.833. No single signal
  transfers; the multi-feature model does.}
  \label{fig:signal}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Seam repair}
Table~\ref{tab:seam} reports the repair sweep and Fig.~\ref{fig:seam} visualises it. A 25\,\% blend reduces the boundary seam by $25\,\%$
($0.614 \rightarrow 0.460$; $\Delta = -0.153$, CI $[-0.162, -0.145]$) and \emph{improves} latent fidelity
($0.761 \rightarrow 0.754$; $\Delta = -0.0062$, CI $[-0.0084, -0.0039]$); a hard anchor removes the seam
entirely but costs fidelity ($0.799$). The independent flicker endpoint moves the other way: the coarse
mode flickers \emph{less} than fine ($0.0148$ vs.\ $0.0189$), and the repair raises flicker to $0.0218$,
above \emph{both}. The repair therefore trades a boundary discontinuity for interior flicker, and we
report the trade rather than a single metric. This is why an endpoint independent of the compared modes
is mandatory: optimising the seam alone would have hidden the cost.

\subsection{Drift propagates on one GPU and reverses on the other}
Figure~\ref{fig:drift} shows drift trajectories for three chains: all-fine, all-coarse, and one coarse
segment followed by fine. On T4 ($n = 32$ prompts) the one-coarse chain remains above the all-fine chain
for the rest of the rollout, with a significant elevation at four of five tail segments
($+0.031$, $+0.030$, $+0.021$, $+0.024$); the fourth excludes zero only marginally (lower bound
$0.000$), so we treat ``four of five'' as an upper bound. Coarse error therefore \emph{propagates}. One
caveat: part of the elevation may be a \emph{switch-seam} effect rather than propagated coarse error,
because on T4 the all-coarse chain lies \emph{below} the all-fine chain at five of six segments --- the
coarse mode alone is not uniformly worse. On A100 the coarse chain falls \emph{below} the fine chain;
that arm rests on $n = 8$ prompts, which we record as a limitation. Drift is approximately linear, and a
transition model fitted on $(d_t, \text{mode}, t)$ predicts the next drift level at $R^2 = 0.944$ only
because drift is smooth; the granularity choice adds nothing ($R^2 = 0.008$), so a lookahead controller
has little headroom.

\subsection{The regime map is empty: adaptivity does not pay here}
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} places every verified policy in the cost--quality plane, and
Table~\ref{tab:policies} reports the numbers. On T4 with the two-step pair, fixed fine is cheaper but the
coarse mode is closer to the reference (0.752 against 0.807) at $3\%$ extra cost.\footnote{The two T4
fine-$s_2$ baselines ($281.2$\,s in the $s_2/s_2$ run and $299.9$\,s in the $s_2/s_1$ run) come from two
separate capture runs of 16 prompts each, and differ by run-to-run wall-clock variance.} On A100, and on
T4 with a one-step coarse schedule, fixed coarse is both cheaper and closer to the reference. In all
three cases the adaptive region of Eq.~\eqref{eq:regime} is empty, so a fixed mode is optimal under
Eq.~\eqref{eq:objective}: at T4 $s_2/s_2$ the cost- and quality-optimal modes differ (fine is cheaper,
coarse is closer) whereas at the other two operating points they coincide. This is the paper's central
finding, and it is a \emph{design rule}: a practitioner computes $r$ and $\Delta$ once and deploys
accordingly.

An oracle router that knows the true coarse-unsafe label in advance bounds what any signal could buy. It
is never \emph{cost}-optimal --- it always spends more seconds than the cheapest fixed mode
(T4 $s_2/s_2$: $287.3$ vs.\ $281.2$; T4 $s_2/s_1$: $240.0$ vs.\ $218.3$; A100: $49.8$ vs.\ $43.9$) ---
because it must pay both for the decision and for escalated segments. It does buy \emph{quality} (A100:
$+5.9$\,s for $-0.014$ endpoint distance), but it never changes which fixed mode is cost-optimal. The
signal decides where on the frontier a deployment sits; the regime decides which mode it should start
from.

\subsection{A third endpoint agrees: decoded pixels}
Table~\ref{tab:policies} rests on the latent endpoint. To check that the conclusion is not an artefact of
that metric we re-ran the matched design on T4 for a matched subset of $n = 8$ prompts (32 segments,
the same protocol as Table~\ref{tab:protocol}) and decoded every segment. This run uses the $s_2/s_1$
pair of Table~\ref{tab:summary} (fine $c_{1\text{-}1}$ at two steps, coarse $c_{3\text{-}3}$ at one step);
in this smaller run the coarse mode is both cheaper ($0.711$ of the fine cost --- the $s_2/s_1$ cost
ratio \emph{of this $8$-prompt run}, against $r = 0.728$ in the $16$-prompt run of
Table~\ref{tab:summary}) and closer by decoded pixels ($0.2511$ against $0.2686$). Three independent
endpoints --- latent, decoded-pixel and flicker --- therefore agree that the coarse mode is not worse,
which licenses the fixed-mode conclusion. The decoded-pixel endpoint was run on T4 only, which we record
as a limitation.

\paragraph{A practitioner recipe.}
(1) Measure $r$ and $\Delta$ on the target accelerator and schedule. (2) If $r>1$ use fine; if $r<1$ and
$\Delta\le 0$ use coarse; only in the remaining region deploy a router. (3) If a router is deployed,
prefer an intrinsic probe, calibrate its threshold on the target GPU, and never certify the result with
the endpoint used to fit it.

\paragraph{Limitations and threats to validity.}
The paper's own thesis is that everything here is regime-dependent, so the scope of the claims matters.
(i)~\emph{One backbone}: a single causal distilled diffusion model (Wan2.1-1.3B, Causal-rCM); the regime
map is a method, not a claim about other backbones.
(ii)~\emph{Two accelerators, one schedule family}: the probe inversion
($0.798 \rightarrow 0.143$) is direct evidence that \emph{no} number here should be assumed to transfer.
(iii)~\emph{Small samples and a single seed}: the A100 drift arm uses $n = 8$ prompts against $n = 32$ on
T4, so the intervals are wide.
(iv)~\emph{Asymmetric endpoint coverage}: the decoded-pixel endpoint was run on T4 only.
(v)~\emph{No step-allocation or chunk-shape baseline}: the closest published alternatives are discussed
but not re-implemented.
(vi)~\emph{One signal family}: only prefill activations and sampler update magnitudes are probed. None of
these affects the central negative result --- the coarse mode was not worse in quality at any measured
operating point --- but each bounds its generalisation.

\paragraph{Generalisation.}
The \emph{procedure} generalises: measure $r$ and $\Delta$ on the target (accelerator, schedule); audit
any candidate signal \emph{per GPU} and by direct AUROC; prefer a multi-feature model when single signals
invert; never certify with the endpoint used to fit. The \emph{numbers} do not.

\paragraph{Ablations.}
From stored results: the 30-block sweep underlying Fig.~\ref{fig:signal} gives AUROC $0.71$--$0.80$ for
every block on T4, so the result is not an artefact of block $11$; and the escalation-rate sweep
underlying Table~\ref{tab:policies} traces the whole $\rho$--quality frontier, where no point beats the
fixed policy.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Adaptive temporal granularity for causal video world models is not a universal win; it is a decision that
depends on the accelerator, the schedule and the endpoint. We measured the three preconditions on two
GPUs, built an intrinsic signal that needs no external oracle, repaired the switch seam without training,
and derived a regime map that states exactly when a router is worth deploying. At every operating point we
measured, the coarse mode was never worse in quality --- on the latent endpoint for both GPUs and on the
decoded-pixel and flicker endpoints for T4 --- so a fixed policy is optimal under the stated objective and
the adaptive region is empty. Where the coarse mode was also dearer (T4 $s_2/s_2$), fine is the
cost-optimal choice even though coarse is closer to the reference. We regard the boundary analysis --- the
non-transfer of signals, the linearity of drift, and the context requirement of decision-model critics ---
as the paper's most reusable output, because each boundary is a design constraint that can be checked
before deployment.
